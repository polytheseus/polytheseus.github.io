Mileage allowance: 55p a mile from April 2026, and 35p above 10,000 miles from April 2027

The 45p rate for business miles in your own car or van went up to 55p on 6 April 2026, and Budget 2026 announced that the rate above 10,000 miles goes from 25p to 35p in April 2027. Here's which rate goes on which return, and a free calculator for your own miles.

The short answer. For business miles in your own car or van you can claim 55p a mile for the first 10,000 miles and 25p after that in the 2026 to 2027 tax year (it was 45p and 25p up to 5 April 2026). From 6 April 2027 the rate above 10,000 miles is due to rise to 35p. Motorcycles stay at 24p and bicycles (employees only) at 20p.

The same rates apply to sole traders using simplified expenses and to employees using their own car for work. The 2025 to 2026 return you file by 31 January 2027 still uses 45p.

Which rate goes on which return

  • 2025 to 2026 return (due online by 31 January 2027): 45p and 25p.
  • 2026 to 2027 return (due by 31 January 2028): 55p and 25p.
  • 2027 to 2028: 55p and 35p, if the regulations go through as announced.
  • Accounts that don't end on 31 March or 5 April: HMRC's manual says to work out the period's vehicle expenses with the rates for the tax year you're taxing, then apportion the profit as usual. So a year to 31 December 2026 is worked out at 45p for the part that falls into 2025 to 2026 and at 55p for the part in 2026 to 2027 (BIM75005, with examples).

Things that catch people out

  • The 10,000 miles are shared. If your business runs two vans, the higher rate covers 10,000 miles across both, not 10,000 each (ITTOIA s94F(3)). For employees, all the cars and vans you use for one job count together.
  • Once you pick the mileage rate for a vehicle, you keep it as long as that vehicle is in the business. If you've ever claimed capital allowances on it, or deducted its cost under the cash basis (vans and motorcycles), you can't switch to the mileage rate for it.
  • It covers everything about running the vehicle: fuel or charging, insurance, repairs, servicing, road tax, MOT and depreciation. Don't claim any of those as well. Parking, tolls and congestion charges on business trips go on top.
  • Commuting isn't business. Travel between home and a regular base (your shop, unit or office) doesn't count. Trips to customers' sites, suppliers and the merchant do. Trades who work from home and go out to different jobs can usually claim the drive to each job; HMRC's Business Income Manual (BIM37600 onwards) goes through the cases.
  • Electric and hybrid cars get the same rates. The mileage rate doesn't care what fuel you use, which makes it generous for a cheap-to-run car.
  • Limited companies can't use simplified expenses. A director using their own car is an employee for this: the company can pay up to the approved amount tax-free.

Mileage rate or actual costs?

Simplified expenses suit most people with an ordinary car or a van that's mainly for work and modest running costs: one number, a log, done. Actual costs (the business share of all the running costs, plus capital allowances on the vehicle) can come out higher when you've bought an expensive van outright, when the vehicle is used almost only for business, or when running costs are high. Because the choice sticks for the life of the vehicle, compare both before your first claim. HMRC's simplified expenses checker does the comparison, and a bookkeeper or accountant can too.

For employees: claiming Mileage Allowance Relief

If your employer pays you less than the approved amount (or nothing), you can claim tax relief on the difference. Many employers still paid 45p after April 2026: that leaves 10p a mile on your first 10,000 miles to claim.

  • Keep a log: the date, where from and to, why, and the miles, for every work journey. HMRC asks for copies when you claim.
  • Claim online on gov.uk, or by post, for the current tax year and the 4 before it. If you file a Self Assessment return, claim on the return instead.
  • You get tax back at your rate (20% of the relief for a basic-rate taxpayer), and it can't be more than the tax you paid that year.
  • If your employer pays more than the approved amount, the extra is taxable and should go through payroll or on a P11D.

Source: gov.uk tax relief for vehicles you use for work.

Keep a log that stands up

The claim is only as good as the record behind it. For each business journey note the date, the start and end points, the reason (the customer or job) and the miles. Keep the odometer reading at the start and end of each tax year too. A notes app or a spreadsheet is fine; you don't need special software.

FAQ

Has the 45p mileage rate gone up?

Yes. From 6 April 2026 the approved rate for the first 10,000 business miles in a car or van is 55p, up from 45p, for both the self-employed (simplified expenses) and employees (approved mileage allowance payments). Above 10,000 miles it stays at 25p until April 2027.

What are the HMRC mileage rates for 2026/27?

Cars and vans 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles and 25p after that; motorcycles 24p; bicycles 20p (employees). Employers can also pay 5p a mile per passenger tax-free.

Is the mileage rate going up to 35p in 2027?

Budget 2026 announced that the rate for business mileage above 10,000 miles rises from 25p to 35p from 6 April 2027, for employees and for the self-employed. The first 10,000 miles stay at 55p. It will be set by regulations later in the 2026 to 2027 year, so treat it as announced until then.

Which mileage rate do I use on my 2025/26 tax return?

45p for the first 10,000 business miles and 25p after that. The 55p rate only applies from the 2026 to 2027 tax year. If your accounting year doesn't end on 31 March or 5 April, see the apportionment note above.

Is the 10,000 miles per vehicle?

No. For the self-employed, the 10,000 miles at the higher rate are shared by all the business's cars and vans in the period. For employees, all the cars and vans used for one employment count together. Motorcycles are 24p a mile at any mileage.

Can I claim mileage and fuel?

Not for the same vehicle. The mileage rate covers fuel or electricity and all other running costs. You can still claim parking, tolls, congestion charges and other travel such as train fares.
