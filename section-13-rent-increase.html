Section 13 rent increase: when can the new rent start?

Since the Renters' Rights Act came in on 1 May 2026, there's only one way to put the rent up for a private tenancy in England: a Section 13 notice on Form 4A. Get the start date wrong and the notice may not count. This free calculator works out the earliest valid date and the last day to serve it.

The short answer. Fill in Form 4A and serve it on your tenant. The new rent can start on the latest of these: 2 months after the notice is served, 52 weeks after the tenancy began, and 52 weeks (sometimes 53) after the last increase took effect. It must also fall on the first day of a rent period, so a tenancy that started on the 20th has its new rent start on a 20th.

Rent review clauses in tenancy agreements no longer work, and you need Form 4A even if the tenant has agreed to the increase (gov.uk landlord guide).

The four date rules

RuleWhat it meansWhere it comes from
2 months' noticeCounted from the day the notice is served, not the day you sign it. Allow time for post.Housing Act 1988 s13(2)(a); Form 4A Note A2
Not in the first yearThe first increase can't start until 52 weeks after the tenancy began.s13(2)(b); Note A3
Once every 52 weeks52 weeks after the last increase took effect. If that date would be more than six days before the anniversary of your first increase since 11 February 2003, it's 53 weeks.s13(2)(c), (3A), (3B); Note A3
On a rent dayThe new rent starts at the beginning of a rent period: the same day of the month for monthly rent, the same weekday for weekly rent.s13(2); Note A4

The 52-week rule counts increases from before 1 May 2026 too, whether they came from an old Form 4 or a rent review clause. A review-clause increase agreed before 1 May 2026 that was due to start after it doesn't apply. Form 4A also says the rent period must be a month or less.

Worked examples

All of these were run through the calculator above.

  1. The gov.uk example. A monthly tenancy that pays on the 1st had an increase from 1 February 2026. 52 weeks later is Sunday 31 January 2027, so the next rent day, 1 February 2027, is the earliest the next increase can start. Serve the notice by hand by 1 December 2026.
  2. Posting it this week. The tenancy started on 20 March 2024; the rent went up on 20 January 2025 and again on 20 January 2026. Posted on Thursday 29 October 2026, the notice counts as served on Monday 2 November, so 2 months takes you to 2 January 2027. 52 weeks after the last increase is 19 January, so the next rent day, 20 January 2027, works. Post it by 18 November to keep that date. The increase after that could start on 20 January 2028.
  3. A new tenancy. Started on 15 May 2026. However early you serve, the first increase can't start until 52 weeks have passed (14 May 2027), so the earliest is the rent day after that: 15 May 2027.
  4. The 31 December trap. Served by hand on 31 December 2026, "2 months later" would be 31 February, which doesn't exist. The calculator plays safe and uses 1 March 2027, which also suits a tenancy that pays on the 1st.
  5. A weekly tenancy and the 53-week rule. Rent is due on Mondays. The first increase was Monday 6 April 2020 and the last one Monday 30 March 2026. 52 weeks on is 29 March 2027, eight days before the anniversary of the first increase, so it becomes 53 weeks: Monday 5 April 2027.

Filling in and serving Form 4A

  • Use the new form. Form 4A (dated 05.26 in the footer) is for private tenancies from 1 May 2026. The old Form 4 is now for social housing. Don't change the wording: gov.uk says a changed form may not be valid.
  • Question 4.2: the date the tenancy started. 4.3: the date the most recent increase took effect, or blank. 4.4: the first increase after 11 February 2003; it decides whether 52 or 53 weeks apply.
  • 4.5 and 4.1: the rent per rent period (per month, per week). 4.7: only bills included in the rent that the tenant doesn't pay separately, such as council tax or a fixed service charge.
  • Names: every joint tenant's name. Joint landlords either all sign or agree that one signs for everyone.
  • Serving it: use a method your tenancy agreement lists. If it doesn't list one, hand it over, leave it at the tenant's address or send it by registered post. Email counts only if the agreement allows it. Keep proof: a photo of the form going through the door, the post receipt, or the sent email.
  • Talk first. gov.uk suggests discussing the increase with your tenant before you serve. A short letter with the form (the calculator writes one) helps.

How much can the rent go up?

There's no percentage cap. What limits it is the open market rent: what the home would let for on the open market on the same terms. If your tenant thinks your figure is higher than that, they can apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) before the new rent's start date (gov.uk; the tenant's fee is £47, with help available).

  • The tribunal can set a rent lower than yours, but never higher than the one in your notice.
  • The tenant keeps paying the old rent until the tribunal decides. The new rent then starts from the date in your notice or, if that's passed, the next rent day after the decision. No backdating.
  • If that would cause the tenant undue hardship, the tribunal can delay the start by up to 2 months.
  • The tribunal can take the property's condition into account. Fix outstanding repairs before you ask for more.
  • Tenants can also challenge the starting rent in the first 6 months of a tenancy.

Check comparable homes on the property portals before you pick a figure, and be ready to explain it. If your tenant claims Universal Credit or Housing Benefit, Form 4A reminds them to report the change; the Local Housing Allowance rates show what their help covers.

Common mistakes

  • Counting 2 months from the date on the form. It's from when the tenant receives it. Posting on a Friday before a bank holiday can cost you nearly a week.
  • Picking a date that isn't a rent day. The 1st of the month is wrong for a tenancy that pays on the 20th.
  • Relying on the rent review clause. It stopped working on 1 May 2026. Use Form 4A.
  • "We agreed it, so I don't need the form." You do: gov.uk says the Section 13 process applies every time, even when the tenant agrees.
  • Forgetting an increase from before May 2026. It still starts the 52-week clock.
  • Using the old Form 4 or editing the form's wording.
  • Emailing it when the tenancy agreement doesn't allow notices by email.

What this doesn't cover

  • Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own rules: Wales (form RHW12), Scotland, Northern Ireland.
  • Social housing (Form 4), assured agricultural occupancies (Form 5A) and old regulated tenancies (gov.uk) follow different routes.
  • Lodgers in your own home aren't assured tenants: agree changes with them directly.
  • A notice served before 1 May 2026 on the old Form 4 still runs on its own terms.

FAQ

How much notice do I need to give to increase rent in England?

At least two months on Form 4A, for every private assured tenancy since 1 May 2026, whatever the rent period. The other three rules above decide the earliest date too.

Can I still use a rent review clause?

No. Since 1 May 2026 a clause that says the rent will or may go up has no effect. Every increase goes through Section 13.

How often can I increase the rent?

Once a year at most: 52 weeks after the last increase took effect (53 in some cases), and not in the first 52 weeks of the tenancy.

Is there a cap on rent increases?

No fixed cap. The tenant can ask the tribunal to decide the open market rent before the start date. It can't go higher than your figure, and the tenant pays the old rent until it decides.

Can I serve Form 4A by email?

Only if your tenancy agreement allows notices by email. Otherwise by hand, at the address or by post, with proof.

What if I get the date wrong?

A start date that's too soon or not on a rent day may make the notice invalid, and the tenant can challenge it. Serve a new notice with a correct date rather than relying on a doubtful one.
